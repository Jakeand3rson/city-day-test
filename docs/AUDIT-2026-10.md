# City Day: Audit and Recommendations

Date: 2026-10-06
Scope: the whole repo at `c7cd825` (index.html, qa-verify.mjs, README, package.json), the commit history, and the running behavior of the stop-picking engine across a set of probe scenarios.

---

## 1. Summary

City Day is a single-page, no-backend test: a creator describes a day for two people in St. Petersburg, FL, the page builds a short list of verified-open places for that date, checks the NWS forecast, and produces a share link. The recipient gets a "mystery walk" that reveals stops by mood.

The core idea is good and has a real wedge: a day given as a gift, with a reveal, grounded in places that are actually open on that date. Most date-planning tools are lists. This one is closer to a present.

The implementation is a clean prototype, but four things limit what this test can teach you:

1. The product promise and the engine disagree. The copy says "a nudge, not a schedule … one or two things," and the history says "keep the day to a walk, a shop, and a meal." On the dates with events, the engine returns 6 to 8 stops no matter what the note says, and an empty note still yields 6.
2. The data has a two-week shelf life. Every dated event is October 10, 11 or 17, 2026. After October 17, most days fall back to 2 to 4 standing-hours stops. The test window is about 11 days from today.
3. There is no feedback loop. It is a friends-and-family test with no way to learn whether anyone opened the link, went, or liked it.
4. The "note shapes the day" claim is regex keyword matching that ignores negation ("no seafood" still lands a seafood restaurant).

Recommendation in one line: before October 10, fix the stop count and the handful of bugs below, add a one-tap feedback prompt, and get 10 to 20 real links sent. Then decide whether the next build is a data pipeline (to escape the two-week shelf life) or an LLM interpreter on a curated catalog (to make the note matter). Do not build both at once.

---

## 2. What the product is today

- Surface: one `index.html` (about 1,960 lines: CSS, three copies of the same inline SVG, a hand-coded catalog of 19 places, the engine, and UI). Hosted on GitHub Pages.
- Creator flow: About screen, then a form (names, occasion, kind of day, date, drinking, mystery vs plan, must-sees vs gems, dollar budget, free-text note), then a creator desk with share link, "Open as guest," note edit and rebuild.
- Recipient flow: `#play/<base64 plan>` opens a landing card, then moods (morning, afternoon, evening) that reveal stops, plus a "Show the day" escape hatch. Skips are stored on the recipient's phone only.
- Data: 19 hand-entered places with hours functions, closure lists for 2026, and source links. Five are dated one-offs (Oct 10, Oct 10/11, Oct 17).
- Weather: NWS `api.weather.gov`, one fixed point downtown, daytime period for the chosen date, "wet" if the text mentions rain, storms or thunder.
- State: localStorage for the creator's plans; the share link encodes the plan's inputs, not its resolved stops.
- Tests: `qa-verify.mjs`, 13 DOM-level checks run through linkedom.

---

## 3. What is working

- Truthfulness as a design rule. "No weather was invented," source links on every stop, and "Nothing verified is open for this date" instead of filler. This is the right instinct and it is the product's best differentiator. Keep it as a hard constraint in every future version.
- The gift mechanic. Splitting the creator desk from the `#play/` recipient view (latest commit) is the most important product decision in the repo. It turns a planner into something you give.
- Restraint in scope. City fixed, no accounts, no payment, no bookings. Correct for a test.
- Escaping and input validation. All rendered strings go through `esc()`, and `decodePlan` whitelists enums and clamps names.
- Commit discipline. Small commits with plain-English intent. Easy to audit.
- The QA script tests real behaviors (mystery leaks, weather redraw loop, museum avoidance), not trivia.

## 4. Product findings: promise vs behavior

### 4.1 The day is too long, and the note barely changes it

Probe results (from running `pickStops` directly):

| Scenario | Stops returned |
|---|---|
| Oct 10, easy anniversary, empty note, $100 | market, MFA, festival, pumpkin patch, ARTpool, Palehorse (6) |
| Oct 10, easy, "coffee and a walk", $60 | Kahwa, market, MFA, festival, pumpkin patch, ARTpool, Palehorse (7) |
| Oct 17, easy, "easy walking", $100 | Boyd Hill 7:30am volunteer planting, market, Morean, Pier, ARTpool, Good Folk Fest (ticketed) (6) |
| Oct 10, celebrate, "vegetarian dinner, no seafood", $300 | 8 stops including Stillwaters (seafood-tagged) and two lunches, no dinner |
| Tue Oct 13, empty note, $200 | Pier, ARTpool (2) |
| Sat Mar 6 2027, "coffee" | Kahwa, market, Pier, ARTpool (4) |

Why:
- Every dated event and every pinned place is added unconditionally, before the note is consulted (`index.html:1172-1174`). On Oct 10 that is six stops before the note is read.
- The filler loop caps at 6 or 7 (`index.html:1243`), but the dated and pinned set is not bound by it, and the wet-weather museum rule adds stops after the cap (`index.html:1255-1259`).
- An "easy" anniversary on Oct 17 gets a 7:30am, 18-and-up, reservation-required volunteer shift. That is the opposite of easy.

Fix: decide the shape of a day and enforce it. Suggested: one anchor (a dated event or a must-see), one wander (walk, shop, gallery), one meal, and optionally one "something you might have missed." Dated events should compete for the anchor slot on fit, not be forced in. Hard cap at 3 to 4.

### 4.2 The note is keyword matching, and negation is ignored

`noteWants` (`index.html:1103-1121`) and `tagScore` (`index.html:1132-1139`) look for words. "No seafood" contains "seafood," which boosts Stillwaters. "Vegetarian" triggers the food flag but no place is tagged vegetarian. Only museums have an avoid rule (`index.html:1096-1101`).

The Learn More dialog tells people to write "no seafood" and "what to skip," so the UI invites exactly the input the engine mishandles.

Short-term fix: a general avoid parser (no/skip/avoid/not/without + term) applied to tags, and add dietary tags to the meal entries. Medium-term: see 6.2.

### 4.3 The budget is not really checked

The copy says the dollar amount "gets checked against parking, food, and a ticketed stop" (`index.html:386`). In practice it is three thresholds: under $80 drops mid-priced meals, under $150 drops nice dinners and most tickets, under $300 limits museums to one (`index.html:1141-1146`, `1236`, `1261-1274`). Nothing is summed. A $100 day can still include a Good Folk Fest ticket, a museum ticket on a wet day, and a sit-down lunch.

Fix: either give each stop a rough per-couple cost and actually sum it against the cap, or soften the copy to "a lower number keeps the pricier stops off." Given the product's truthfulness rule, the copy cannot overclaim.

### 4.4 "The link is the same day for anyone" is not guaranteed

The share link encodes the inputs (`shareUrl`, `index.html:1456`), and the recipient's browser recomputes the stops. The result depends on:
- the forecast at the time of opening (wet weather adds museums, `index.html:1255`), and the forecast is only available about 7 days out, so a link made two weeks ahead will produce a different day once the forecast appears;
- whatever catalog is deployed when the link is opened, so any edit to `STOPS` or the hours functions silently changes every outstanding gift.

For a gift product this matters: the creator approved a specific day, and the recipient may see a different one. Fix: encode the resolved stop IDs (and a catalog version) in the link, and treat weather as an annotation ("only if it clears," "indoor backup: X") rather than a re-plan.

### 4.5 Mystery is a nice frame, but it is cosmetic

The recipient can tap "Show the day" at any point, and the full plan is base64 in the URL. That is fine for friends and family, but it means the reveal is a UI choice, not a mechanic. The stronger version is time-released reveal (the next stop unlocks when the previous one is marked done, or at its start time), which also gives you the engagement signal you currently lack (see 4.6).

### 4.6 There is no way to learn from the test

No analytics, no "did you go," no "how was it." A test without a measurement is a demo. Minimum viable instrumentation:
- A privacy-light counter (GoatCounter or Plausible) for: form started, day made, link copied, play link opened, mood tapped, Show the day, "Not for us," Get directions.
- A one-question prompt in play mode after the date has passed: "Did you go? Which stop was best?" posting to a form or a free endpoint.
- Track the "Not for us" IDs; they are the cheapest signal you have about catalog quality, and today they never leave the phone.

### 4.7 The catalog is thin off-peak and expires soon

19 places, 5 of them one-off events in a 7-day window. A random Tuesday yields 2 stops. Closure lists end in 2026 (`index.html:524-528`). Dalí special hours are hard-coded for three dates (`index.html:530-536`). This is the binding constraint on the whole product: the value proposition ("things actually open, including something you might have missed") is only as good as the dated-event data, and that data is entered by hand into JavaScript.

## 5. Bugs and defects

Ordered by user impact.

1. "Open as guest" does nothing visible. `index.html:1862-1866` calls `location.assign` with a URL that differs only by its hash. Browsers treat that as a fragment navigation, not a reload, and there is no `hashchange` listener, so the creator desk stays on screen. If the creator then reloads, they land in play mode on their own phone and the only way back to the desk is `?create`, which is not surfaced anywhere. Fix: add a `hashchange` handler that re-runs `boot()`, and add a visible "Back to your desk" link in play mode when the plan ID exists in the creator's local store.

2. The wet-weather line names closed or dropped museums. `weatherLine` (`index.html:1363`) always says "The Dalí and the MFA stay on the day." On a wet Monday with a $100 budget the actual stops are Morean, Dalí, Pier: the MFA is closed Mondays and the budget rule would have dropped a second museum anyway. Derive the line from the actual stops.

3. The QA script crashes on Node 21+. `qa-verify.mjs:56` assigns `navigator` onto the linkedom window, which proxies to `globalThis`, where Node 21+ defines a getter-only `navigator`. Result: `TypeError: Cannot set property navigator`. With that line removed, all 13 checks pass on Node 22. Fix with `Object.defineProperty`, or drop the assignment and stub `navigator.clipboard` separately. Also wire it to `npm test` (`package.json` still has the default failing test script) and pin a Node version in `package.json` `engines`.

4. Negated preferences boost the thing they negate (see 4.2). "No seafood" adds a seafood restaurant.

5. Wet weather can push the day past its own cap and over budget (`index.html:1255-1259`), and does so even for "easy" days where museums are otherwise filtered (`index.html:1235`).

6. Stray closing tag. `index.html:473` has an extra `</section>`. Harmless in browsers, but it will confuse any future tooling or templating.

7. Hard-coded "known dates" for the gap line (`index.html:1656`) duplicate the catalog. Derive from `STOPS.filter(s => s.dated)` so adding an event cannot leave the copy wrong.

8. No length limit on the note in `decodePlan` (`index.html:1433`). A 500-character note makes a ~1,000-character link. Not a security issue (everything is escaped), but long links break in some SMS clients and look suspicious. Cap at ~400 characters in the form and the decoder.

9. Past dates are accepted by the form (`index.html:354`, no `min`). The day still builds, and the weather call returns "not out yet," which is misleading for a past date.

10. The form error is not announced to screen readers (`index.html:329`). Add `role="alert"` or `aria-live="polite"`.

11. Setting `User-Agent` on a browser `fetch` (`index.html:1339`) is ignored by Chrome and can trigger a CORS preflight elsewhere. The fallback covers it, but it costs an extra request on every first load. Drop it in the browser; NWS does not require it from browsers.

12. Dead fields carried in every link: `budget` (legacy buttons), `custom` (removed field), `city` (always overwritten). Remove them from new plans and keep decoding them for old links.

## 6. Engineering and architecture

### 6.1 Near-term structure (keep it static, make it maintainable)

- Split `STOPS` into `data/stops.json` (or a JS module) with a schema: id, name, address, geo, tags, avoid-tags, cost per couple, category (anchor/wander/meal/sweet), hours rules, closures, source, last-verified date. Today hours are code (`function (d) {...}`); move them to data (weekly hours plus exception dates) so a non-developer, or a script, can edit them.
- Make `pickStops` pure: inputs are plan, catalog, and an optional weather object. It currently reads the global `weatherByDate` (`index.html:1255`), which is part of why the creator and the recipient can see different days.
- Encode resolved stop IDs plus a catalog version in share links (4.4).
- Deduplicate the SVG (three copies) into one `<symbol>` with `<use>`.
- Keep vanilla JS and GitHub Pages. There is no reason to add a framework at this stage.
- Add a GitHub Action that runs `npm test` on push.

### 6.2 Where AI actually helps, and where it does not

The note box is the product's personalization surface, and regex will not get much better than it is now. This is the natural place for an LLM, under a strict grounding contract:

- The model interprets the note into structured preferences (wants, avoids, dietary, pace, start time, transport) and ranks catalog IDs. It never generates places, hours or prices. The output is validated against the catalog; anything unknown is dropped.
- It can write the "why" line from the note and the stop's verified facts, which today is templated and reads the same for everyone.
- This requires a small serverless function to hold the API key (Cloudflare Worker or similar), rate limited, with no storage of notes beyond what you choose to log for evaluation.
- Keep the current deterministic engine as the fallback when the call fails or times out, and as the test oracle.

The other AI opportunity is upstream: extracting dated events from the sources you already cite (St. Pete Catalyst boards, the Pier, the Morean calendar, Creative Clay) into the catalog schema, with a human approving each one before it ships. That is the path out of the two-week shelf life, and it is where any durable advantage would come from. Ingestion with human verification is a better first AI investment than note interpretation, because the catalog bounds everything else.

### 6.3 Privacy

The share link contains both names and the full note, in base64 (which is encoding, not protection). It will sit in message histories and link previews. For friends and family this is acceptable; before any wider launch, move the plan behind a short opaque ID on a backend or at minimum say on the creator desk that the link contains the names and note.

## 7. Usability: a compass, not a map

The current day view works like a list you read: cards with paragraphs, each with a "Get directions" button that hands off to Google Maps. That makes the phone the guide, and it keeps people looking at it.

The overlanding version makes the phone a compass. It tells you which way to head and what to watch for, then gets out of the way. Overlanders don't navigate turn by turn. They have a bearing, a place to end up, a few optional waypoints, and a safety margin. Everything in between is the trip.

One test for every feature: could the day still work if the phone went in a pocket after breakfast? If a feature only works while someone stares at it, cut it.

### 7.1 Plan the day as a route, not a list of stops

Replace the 6-stop list with a simple shape:

- Where the day starts.
- Where the day lands: the evening anchor, such as dinner, sunset or a show.
- One or two optional waypoints along the way.
- A corridor between them, like "the waterfront side of Beach Drive toward the Pier" or "Central Ave west through the Edge District."

The corridor is where the journey happens. Give each leg one or two "look for" prompts: a specific mural, pelicans off the end of the Pier, the block with the record shops. That makes the walk itself the content without adding more stops.

This also fixes the "too many stops" problem in 4.1. A route with two waypoints feels complete, and a list of two feels thin.

### 7.2 One screen during the day: the Now card

When someone opens the link during the day, they see a single card, not the itinerary:

- What's next, and roughly which way: "The Pier, about 12 minutes on foot, toward the water."
- The one constraint that matters: "Market closes at 2."
- One line of why.
- Two small actions: "Something else nearby" and "Head home."

The full day stays one tap away, but it is not the default.

The card knows the time of day. This works without GPS, because the clock alone says whether it is morning, afternoon or evening. It also fits the mystery feature: instead of the recipient tapping a mood, "While it's still easy" unlocks in the morning and "After the light drops" unlocks at dusk. The reveal follows the day, which also answers 4.5.

### 7.3 Nudges that reach people without the app

A web page cannot track location in the background or notify reliably. On iOS, push notifications only work after someone adds the site to their home screen, which almost nobody does.

The answer that needs no backend is a calendar file. "Add the day to your calendar" generates a `.ics` file in the browser with 3 or 4 events, each with an alert. Those alerts reach the lock screen and a watch with no app, account or permission prompt, and they work offline once added.

Nudges flag a window that is closing; they do not give instructions:

- "Market wraps up in an hour."
- "Rain likely after 3. Do the outside part first." The NWS hourly forecast (`forecastHourly`) supports this, and it is far more useful than the current single daily "wet" flag.
- "Sunset at 7:12. The Pier is 15 minutes from here."
- "Last seating at BellaBrava is 9:30."

Cap them at 4 a day. More than that and it turns back into a schedule.

The second fallback is a pocket card: a saveable image of the route, the anchors and the way back, for when signal drops.

### 7.4 Detours as the main way to explore

The overlanding instinct is to take the side trail. One interactive feature makes that real: "We have time. What's near?"

- One tap asks for location once, never continuously.
- It returns one thing: open now, within about 10 minutes on foot, and fitting the note.
- It stays on screen until they tap again.

"Not for us" becomes "Swap": one replacement, not an edited list.

Walking times do not need a paid routing service at this scale. Straight-line distance times about 1.3 is accurate enough for a downtown walk. This requires adding coordinates to every catalog entry (see 6.1).

### 7.5 Safety as a margin, not alarms

The safety model is the overlander's: always know your way back, and respect the conditions.

- A way back that is always visible. One tap at the start saves where you parked. "Head home" is always on the Now card and gives the bearing to the car, or a rideshare link.
- Daylight. Calculate sunset in the browser, and make the day land somewhere lit and busy before dark. The end anchor in 7.1 does this naturally.
- Florida conditions. Summer afternoon thunderstorms are almost daily, and lightning is a real risk on the open waterfront and the Pier. Heat index matters on a long walk. Nudge on both, and put shade, water and restrooms along each leg.
- Offline. A small service worker caches the page and the day so it works on bad signal.

Deliberately avoid flagging "unsafe neighborhoods." It is hard to get right, it ages badly, and it has real fairness problems. Steer with positive signals instead: lit, busy routes with open businesses, and days that end before dark. That gets most of the safety value without labeling areas.

### 7.6 Make the setup lighter to match

The form asks for about 10 things before it shows any value. For a nudge product, three are enough:

1. Who, and the occasion.
2. When.
3. The note.

Drinking, must-sees vs hidden gems, mystery vs plan, and budget get sensible defaults. The creator changes them on the creator desk after seeing the first draft. People react to a draft more easily than they fill in a blank form.

### 7.7 Let the journey become the keepsake

Add an optional "We found something" button. It logs a spot that was not in the plan: a name or a photo, with location if they allow it.

After the day, the recipient gets a short recap: the route they walked, the stops they hit, and what they found. That does three things at once:

- The memory makes the gift better.
- It is the "did you go?" signal from 4.6.
- Places people found on their own are the best source of new catalog entries.

### 7.8 Guardrails to keep it simple

- One screen during the day, and "what's next" is never more than one tap away.
- No continuous location tracking. Location is asked for once, only on a tap.
- At most 4 nudges a day, delivered through the calendar rather than an app.
- No accounts.
- The day still works if they never open the phone after the morning.

One tension to decide early: the more the tool steps back, the less you can measure from inside it. The calendar alerts and the "found" button are how you keep learning without making people watch the screen.

### 7.9 Sequence

1. Now card, with mystery unlocking by time of day. The biggest usability gain, and it only changes how existing data is shown.
2. Calendar export with time-based nudges, plus sunset and hourly rain.
3. "Head home" and the parking pin.
4. Route-and-corridor model with "look for" prompts. Real data work, so start with a few routes.
5. "What's near" detours.
6. "We found something" and the recap.
7. The trimmed form.

Steps 1 to 3 need no backend and fit before October 17. Step 4 is where the product starts to feel like a journey rather than a list.

## 8. Strategic view

Being direct about the business, given the goal of a technology venture with a 3 to 5 year exit:

- As a consumer date planner, this is a crowded, low willingness-to-pay category. General tools (maps, review apps, social video, event listings) already answer "what should we do," and they are free. A better itinerary alone will not support a venture outcome.
- The parts with an edge are: (a) verified, dated, local event data that is actually right, and (b) the gift and reveal mechanic. (a) is an operations problem that compounds per city; (b) is a distribution mechanic, because every gift is sent to a new person.
- Paths worth testing, roughly in order of cost:
  1. Gift product (consumer, paid per day). Someone plans and sends a surprise day for an anniversary or birthday. The buyer is the creator; price against flowers or a gift card, not against a free app. Test willingness to pay with a "would you pay $X for this" button before building payments.
  2. Hospitality and concierge (B2B). Boutique hotels, short-term rental hosts and tourism boards want "a good day in our city" content for guests and cannot keep it current. Your verified catalog plus the share-link format is the product; they pay per property per month. This is where the ingestion pipeline becomes the moat.
  3. Local business placement. Revenue, but it conflicts directly with the truthfulness rule that makes the product trustworthy. Only consider it with clear labeling, and only after trust is established.
- What would make me stop: if 15 to 20 real links go out and fewer than half of recipients open past the landing card, or almost nobody reports going, the gift framing is not landing and the effort should move elsewhere. That is a cheap, fast answer, which fits the lean approach.

## 9. Prioritized plan

### Now (before Saturday, October 10)

The highest-value data in the catalog expires on October 17. Ship these first so the test produces signal.

1. Cap the day at 3 to 4 stops with an anchor, wander, meal shape; dated events compete for the anchor (4.1).
2. Fix "Open as guest" with a `hashchange` handler and a "Back to desk" link (bug 1).
3. Derive the wet-weather line from actual stops (bug 2).
4. Add a basic avoid parser for "no / skip / avoid X" across tags (4.2).
5. Add a privacy-light counter and a post-date "Did you go?" prompt (4.6).
6. Fix the QA script for Node 22, wire `npm test`, add a GitHub Action (bug 3).
7. If time allows before October 17: the Now card, calendar nudges, and "Head home" (7.9 steps 1 to 3).
8. Send 15 to 20 links to real couples. Write down what you expect to happen before you send them.

### Next (October to November, after reading the results)

9. Encode resolved stops plus a catalog version in links; make `pickStops` pure (4.4, 6.1).
10. Move the catalog to a data file with a schema, per-couple cost, and last-verified date; sum the budget for real or soften the copy (4.3, 6.1).
11. Add 15 to 25 standing-hours places, with coordinates, so ordinary weekdays produce a full day (4.7).
12. Route-and-corridor model, "What's near" detours, and the trimmed form (7.1, 7.4, 7.6).
13. Pick one of: event ingestion with human review, or LLM note interpretation over the catalog. Recommendation: ingestion first (6.2).

### Later (only if the test says the gift framing works)

14. Small backend: short opaque links, stored plans, server-side LLM calls, feedback storage.
15. "We found something" and the post-day recap (7.7).
16. A willingness-to-pay test for the gift product, and a conversation with two or three local hotels about the B2B version (8).
17. A second city, but only once adding a city is a data task rather than a code task.

## 10. What to measure in this test

| Question | Signal | Rough bar to continue |
|---|---|---|
| Do creators finish? | form started vs day made | over 60% |
| Do they send it? | link copied per day made | over 70% |
| Do recipients engage? | play opened past landing | over 50% |
| Is mystery worth it? | mood tapped vs Show the day | moods used at least as often |
| Is the catalog right? | "Not for us" rate per stop | flag any stop skipped over 30% |
| Did it change the day? | "Did you go?" yes, and which stop was best | at least a third went to one or more stops |

## 11. Open questions for you

1. Who is the customer: the person who makes the day, or the couple who lives it? The answer changes whether you optimize the form or the walk.
2. Is St. Petersburg a test city or the business? A single-city, data-quality-first product and a multi-city platform need different first investments.
3. How much time per week goes to this? The ingestion pipeline is the right long-term move but needs ongoing review time; at 2 to 3 hours per week it must be mostly automated with fast approve/reject.
4. Is the B2B hospitality angle interesting to you? It fits your enterprise background better than consumer growth does, and it has a clearer path to the exit size you are targeting.
